    0. PRESERVATION-LAYER REVIEW
    
    Attack (a): summarization is lossy and unauditable at scale. You compress
    many KB of transcript into one memory card. At 36,826 messages on one
    host, and more across a fleet, no human audits the cards. The failure is
    not lossiness, it is silent lossiness: a card that reads complete while
    quietly dropping the one fact needed in six months. Countermeasure that
    makes this survivable: every card carries provenance (source session id,
    exact byte range, a checksum) so any card is auditable on demand instead
    of at scale, and the source transcript is NEVER deleted until the card
    exists AND the source is quarantined, not hard-deleted. Add a
    low-confidence bucket: cards the summarizer itself flags get held for a
    human, never dropped. But the honest admission: full audited distillation
    at fleet scale costs roughly what the transcript you were deleting cost.
    You are swapping disk for a verification tax. The fatal variant is
    distill as an irreversible redirect, transcript garbled into card, card
    wrong, source gone. That must be prohibited by construction: the card is
    a view, the source is a quarantined artifact until a human accepts the
    card. That is your own report-only law applied one step earlier than you
    planned.
    
    Attack (b): same-agent importance scoring is a conflict of interest. This
    is the strongest attack in the section and it is not fully solvable. The
    agent that accumulated the mess decides which parts of it are vital.
    Every agent is incentivized to preserve its own past outputs and tool
    traces and to be cavalier about the user's actual needs. Worse than
    self-preservation: the score is calibrated to the model's own retrieval,
    not to the user's long-term facts. The design countermeasure is to bound,
    not remove, the conflict: a card's importance is metadata, not destiny
    (promotable and demotable by the human or by observed usage); scoring
    biases to false positives (when in doubt, keep and quarantine, do not
    distill to a low-confidence card); and importance is scored against the
    user's declared profile, not the agent's convenience. The fatal version
    is the design that lets the agent self-certify "nothing vital was lost."
    If the human is not the final gate for any card below a high confidence
    bar, this feature is trust theater and should be cut. Given the roadmap
    makes context-plane the differentiator and calls sweep-then-distill a
    crime, the entire value proposition leans on a human gate that is not yet
    specified. Specify it or abandon the claim.
    
    Attack (c): retrieval decay turns the memory store into the new dump.
    This is the neatest and arguably the most fatal. You are replacing a
    bounded, greppable artifact (a transcript) with an unbounded, scattered
    artifact (cards nobody queries). A 36 MB transcript you can find with one
    grep. Four thousand scored cards no future session ever pulls are
    strictly harder to search than what you deleted. The name Cognexia,
    "memory loss", is the tell: you have named the disease you are injecting
    into your own product. Design countermeasure: the memory store needs the
    opposite law from your /tmp sweeper. It needs retrieval hooks, not
    retention rules. A card is only alive if it is wired into the thing that
    actually reads memory (the agent's memory injection path that rides the
    paid prompt); otherwise it is dead storage by definition. And the
    preserve layer itself needs a sweep: cards with zero fetches in N get
    demoted or re-attested, so the store is not unbounded. The admission: a
    preserved-but-never-retrieved fact is indistinguishable from a deleted
    fact except that you paid more for it. If there is no retrieval
    observability on Cognexia, preservation is decorative deletion and the
    feature is fatal. The fix is cheap: log every card fetch, sweep
    zero-fetch cards. If a team member objects to logging the fetch path,
    that objection is your sign the preserving is for show.
    
    Bottom line for 0: every attack has a countermeasure, but they stack into
    a real operating tax, and the foundational rule is not stated anywhere: a
    fact is only preserved if it is subsequently retrieved. Otherwise the
    preservation layer is a second dump wearing a better name.
    
    1. FATAL FLAWS, RANKED
    
    F1, existential: the differentiator is unbuilt and the shipped half is
    commodity. The host plane, everything v0.1.0 actually does, is a solved
    problem. find /tmp -mtime +7 -delete plus du plus a cron job already does
    it, on every Mac and in every agent stack. Quarantining screenshots and
    pruning cron logs is CCleaner's category, and CCleaner's category is a
    low-margin graveyard you are late to. The only thing that distinguishes
    sweap.it from a shell script is the context plane, and the context plane
    is precisely the part your own laws admit is dangerous: reversible,
    quarantine, human gate, never reimplement DB surgery, report-only for
    judgment. You built the safe, worthless half and shipped it, and describe
    the risky, valuable half as roadmap. As shipped, the product has no
    reason to exist: its audience can already do all of it, and its buyer
    gets a manifest they could have grepped.
    
    F2, doomed market frame: "CCleaner for AI models run on machines." The
    comparison's entire pitch is reclaiming disk on a personal PC the user
    owns. But the scarce resource in an LLM stack is not disk, it is context
    tokens with a per-call price and information loss on deletion. You
    noticed this, the two-plane model is correct, then the design update
    walked the product back to disk. And there is no "machine" in CCleaner
    that the user owns: agent fleets are multi-host, ephemeral,

    have miscalibrated the buyer by a category.

    index actually help, given the model may not attend to all of it

    maps to money, and you do not have one.

    2. SAFETY HOLES
    
    Keep-list TOCTOU: the keep-list (rea, hf_paper) is matched by name at
    inventory. An active work file not on the keep-list can carry an old
    mtime (cp -p, a checkout, a restored file) and get swept. The deny and
    keep lists are checked once, the deletion target is resolved later. Fix:
    re-resolve the deny and keep lists against the actual inode (dev+ino) at
    deletion time, not the name, and refuse to act on any entry whose inode
    changed since inventory. Use lstat, not stat.
    
    Symlink escape: swap a sweepable name for a symlink pointing outside
    /tmp, and a recursive delete or a recursive quarantine-move follows it
    into a real directory and deletes real files. unlink of a symlink itself
    is safe, but any use of shutil or recursion with follow-symlinks true
    walks into the target. Every operation in the sweep must run with
    symlinks=False and lstat, and directories containing symlinks must be
    inventoried as links, not traversed.
    
    Hardlink trap: the fatal one. A file in /tmp hardlinked to a live log or
    a working copy, nlink > 1. You quarantine and then hard-delete
    /tmp/cron.out, and the live log at the other path name silently loses its
    content, because you deleted the inode both names pointed to. Nothing in
    the spec mentions nlink. Fix: refuse to act on any entry with link count
    > 1, or copy-and-dedupe instead of move. It is a two-line stat check and
    it is currently absent. Also nlink > 1 breaks your size accounting (du
    counts it once).
    
    Cross-volume quarantine: os.rename raises EXDEV when source and
    quarantine are on different volumes (APFS firmlinks, separate mounts).
    The implementer falls back to copy-then-delete. A crash between copy and
    delete leaves a truncated quarantine copy and then the original is gone.
    Fix: make the move an explicit copy, fsync, verify byte size or hash,
    then delete only after verification, or refuse cross-volume moves and
    ask. Never trust a move that was actually a copy.
    
    Disk-quota blindness: the /tmp criterion is age, not space. On APFS /tmp
    shares the volume and is rarely the constraint. You will delete many
    young irrelevant files for zero reclaim, or preserve old tiny files while
    a few huge young ones starve the volume. Fix: report actual volume
    pressure, tag entries by size, and act only where age, size, and pressure
    jointly justify it. Sweeping by mtime alone on a non-constrained volume
    is cargo-cult cleanup.
    
    Permission churn and partial-run abort: an unreadable entry or one
    deleted by another process mid-sweep can crash the run, leaving a
    partially quarantined state where the deleted set no longer equals the
    inventoried set, breaking "reversible" and "inventory never lies" at the
    action level. Fix: make the sweep transactional per file, log every
    action, never abort mid-list, and emit a final manifest of what actually
    happened. Your inventory-never-lies law is currently true at emit time
    only; it must be re-verified after the action or it is a marketing slogan.
    
    com.apple.* prefix deny is too blunt in both directions. It
    over-preserves real user files that happen to start with that prefix
    (safe but wrong), and if enforced by regex or case-insensitively it can
    be drifted. More importantly, /tmp on macOS is /private/tmp and holds
    launchd sockets and domain sockets; any size or empty-file heuristic must
    be type-aware, never delete or act on socket, fifo, or symlink entries,
    not just name-prefixed ones.
    
    The live-store race for the context plane: state.db is live behind the
    gateway. A future sweep that truncates or optimizes rows races with the
    writer. Read-only census is fine. Any store surgery requires an explicit
    offline checkpoint first, and you must never mutate a store you are
    concurrently reading. State it as an invariant, not an instruction, since
    the sweep will be a robot calling the sweeper.
    
    3. MEASUREMENT VALIDITY, 85.4%
    
    Chars is not tokens. LENGTH(content) counts UTF-8 code points. Tool
    output is code, JSON, paths, hex, and base64, which tokenize at a
    different chars-per-token ratio than the prose in user and assistant
    messages. The money metric, prompt cost, is tokens. The 85.4% is in
    chars, and the direction of error is not even guaranteed. Cheap fix:
    re-measure with the model's tokenizer over role=tool content versus total
    and report both. If you will not tokenize, state "85.4% of stored message
    bytes, token share unmeasured" and stop letting the number carry the
    weight it currently does.
    
    Stored bytes is not prompt bytes, and this is the damaging one. 85% of
    STORED transcript bytes being tool output does not imply 85% of PROMPT
    bytes are tool output, because the consumption path drops, truncates, or
    compresses old tool outputs when replaying history. The transcript stores
    every tool output ever; a real prompt only carries the recent window,
    with older outputs compressed or dropped. Consequence: your re-payment
    law is true for the fixed overhead (28.7 + 14.7 + 54.3 KB ride every
    call) but false or unestablished for tool-output bytes, which are paid
    once at the turn they happened and mostly never re-paid. The headline
    metric conflates a one-time storage cost with a recurring per-call cost,
    and it is the per-call cost that justifies the product. Cheap fix:
    instrument a session and measure which tool bytes actually re-enter
    prompts, and report that number in the cost column while 85.4% stays in
    the storage column. As written, 85.4% is a storage statistic dressed as
    an economic finding.
    
    One host is not a census. You have a case study of one Mac,
    self-selected. The word census implies a population and a method; you
    have a sample of one. Cheap fix: in the paper call it a day-0 baseline on
    one host, and make the multi-host count a real RQ1 deliverable, not an
    input. Do not let the word census inflate n=1.
    
    role=tool may be the wrong classifier. It captures tool-result bytes but
    misses tool-invocation JSON (request arguments) if stored under another
    role, misses assistant messages that quote tool output into reasoning,
    and can mis-classify any injected context (profile, memory, skills) that
    arrives as a user or system message. The 85.4% is also of CONTENT bytes
    only, excluding fixed overhead, so a reader hears "85% of everything"
    when it is "85% of message content". Cheap fix: state the denominator on
    every emission and validate the classifier against a hand-labeled sample
    of roughly 100 messages. Ten minutes of labeling would tell you whether
    85.4% is even a real number.
    
    4. RQ AUDIT
    
    RQ1, waste composition and growth law. Composition on one host is
    measured and is a toy result: it is a single point. The growth law is not
    measured at all; you have one snapshot, no time series, so there is no
    rate, no exponent, nothing to fit. Cross-host composition is the
    genuinely publishable piece, nobody has published an agent-waste census
    across hosts, but it is currently roadmap. Verdict: composition = toy;
    growth law = publishable but unmeasured; cross-host = the real
    contribution, unbuilt.
    
    RQ2, safe deletion semantics for conversation state. Strongest and most
    publishable, because your design laws already encode a serious answer:
    quarantine, reversible, never reimplement DB surgery, hard-deny,
    human-gate. But it is research only if you define and test the safety
    property with an oracle: for example, deletion preserves the ability to
    answer a held-out set of provenance queries, or never amputates a fact
    needed to substantiate a made claim. Without that oracle, RQ2 is "our
    tool has a dry-run flag", which is not publishable. Verdict: publishable
    only if you operationalize safe as a testable property; the
    archive-never-amputate idea is a restatement of git and append-only log
    behavior and is not novel alone.
    
    RQ3, can an agent self-sweep without corrupting its own memory. Original
    and publishable, this is the heart of section 5 and the best research in
    the program. But it is entangled with RQ4 and with the Cognexia layer, so
    it cannot be answered until you separate "self-sweep performed correctly"
    from "self-sweep survived despite a lossy layer". Verdict: high value,
    publishable, but only after that separation exists.
    
    RQ4, does cleaning measurably improve task performance. The question that
    decides whether any of this matters, and it currently has no method. It
    needs a controlled before/after benchmark on held-out tasks, cleaning
    applied versus not, controlling for model, temperature, seed, and the
    confound that re-running a task improves the agent regardless of
    cleaning. There is a large long-context degradation and RAG
    full-context-versus-retrieval literature that has mostly answered the
    general form. Novelty is only in applying it to an agent's own runtime
    state. Verdict: publishable but marginal, and high risk of a null result
    (if most tool bytes are never replayed, cleaning changes nothing, which
    is F3), at which point you are publishing a null on a product you are
    selling.
    
    Ranking: RQ1 cross-host, then RQ3, then RQ2 if operationalized, then RQ4
    as null-risk. RQ1 single-host composition and all pure host-plane
    behavior are toy.
    
    5. THE SELF-SWEEP PARADOX
    
    The setup makes every finding self-attested: sweap.it is built by and for
    agents, the research log lives in the agent's repo, the agent writing the
    paper is the measured system, and it is the agent running the tool on its
    own store. Where it invalidates:
    
    Contamination: the census reads state.db while Hermes is live, so the
    measuring session writes new messages under test, and 36,826 and 85.4%
    include the census's own traffic. Earlier sweeps can move files the tool
    then reports as clean, making "inventory" self-fulfilling.
    
    Observer-as-subject: the agent decides what is vital and the agent's own
    memory is what is being graded and being swept. The conflict of interest
    in section 0b is not hypothetical here, it is the agent grading its own
    homework while being graded on the homework.
    
    Selective reporting: the agent curates the research log and the paper. An
    outside reviewer cannot audit whether the numbers are representative or
    the cherry-picked happy path. A dramatic 85.4% and a recoverable host
    plane are exactly the numbers a self-reported paper would lead with.
    
    Confounded RQ3: if self-sweep appears to work, the cause may be practice,
    a lucky state, or the cleaning, and the tool and the measured improvement
    share a cause (the agent), so any attribution is regression-to-mean.
    
    Protocol separations that fix it:
    - Run the cleaner and the measured as different identities: the census
    and cleanup execute as a separate, read-only-isolated instrument with its
    own logging, never the agent under test mutating its own store then
    reporting its own health.
    - Freeze the artifact: snapshot state.db and config to a read-only
    baseline before any sweep, and run every before/after benchmark against
    the frozen snapshot, not the live store. This is the single strongest fix
    in the program.
    - Hold-out audit: a second agent or a human, out of the loop,
    independently re-derives the headline numbers from the frozen raw
    snapshot and checks the paper's claims against it, not against the
    agent's summary.
    - Separate writing from running: the process that runs sweap.it on itself
    has a locked config and is a different named instrument from the one that
    compiles results into the paper, both audited against the frozen snapshot.
    - Log the instrument: an append-only, tamper-evident action log of every
    sweep, so inventory-never-lies can be verified after the fact by an
    external auditor instead of trusted from inside.
    
    Without these, RQ3 and the Cognexia "nothing vital lost" guarantee are
    invalid on contact, because a hostile reviewer treats self-certified
    results as unverified by default.
    
    6. VERDICT, THREE FIXES THIS WEEK
    
    1. Repitch on the fixed overhead re-paid every call, 28.7 plus 14.7 plus
    54.3 KB, and demote 85.4% to a storage statistic, because the product
    dies if it is framed as a disk cleaner.
    2. Build the frozen-snapshot before/after benchmark protocol with a
    held-out task set before any more sweep features, because without it the
    value thesis is unfalsifiable.
    3. Make Cognexia optional and human-gated with per-card provenance and
    retrieval observability, zero-fetch cards swept, and no agent
    self-certification of "nothing vital lost", because the conflict of
    interest and retrieval decay are fatal without an external gate.
